\author{Gautam Khosla\\
\texttt{https://github.com/GautamTalksDev/assay-gpu}}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
Algorithm-based fault tolerance detects corrupted matrix multiplications by
comparing a checksum of the product against a checksum recomputed from the
factors, flagging a fault when the residual exceeds a threshold. The classical
threshold is $\tau = Ku$, derived from a dot-product rounding bound that
requires $Ku \leq 0.01$. At bfloat16 with contraction length $K = 4096$,
$Ku \approx 32$, so the bound does not hold and the threshold is not defined.
Prior work has noted this and consequently evaluated checksum ABFT only at
float32. We supply the missing calibration. This paper is a characterization of
a detector under an output-level, post-GEMM perturbation model; it is not a
characterization of faults in silicon, and no rate reported here estimates
fault incidence in deployed hardware. We fit a Generalized Pareto distribution
to $7.99 \times 10^{7}$ clean per-row residuals and extrapolate a threshold at
a per-GEMM false-positive rate of $10^{-6}$, obtaining
$3.1445 \times 10^{-6}$, which sits between the two clean sample maxima we
observed and is $1.17\times$ the larger of the two runs' floor at $n = 500$.
The independence assumption behind the per-GEMM conversion is checked
empirically rather than asserted, at a variance ratio of $0.985$ against a
binomial reference. Against this threshold the detector catches $304$ of $400$
single exponent-bit flips ($76\%$), failing a bar of $90\%$ that was registered
before any data was collected, and catches $0$ of $600$ low-mantissa flips.
Detectability is invariant in $K$ across a $16\times$ range even though the
clean noise floor falls as $K^{-0.4999}$: signal and floor scale together, so
bit position governs detectability and contraction length does not. This
falsified the mechanism the study was designed around. We report four
registered predictions that failed, including that one, and four retractions of
our own earlier findings, two of which were caught by pre-publication audit
rather than by prediction. Code, data, and every pre-registration document with
its commit timestamp are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Hardware that returns a wrong answer without saying so is a documented
production problem. Google reported silent corrupt execution errors traced to
manufacturing defects in CPU cores \citep{hochschild2021cores}, Meta reported
silent data corruption at fleet scale \citep{dixit2021silent}, and both
observed rates higher than software engineers expect. The failure mode is not a
crash. It is a computation that completes and is wrong.

Matrix multiplication is the natural place to look for this in machine learning
workloads, and algorithm-based fault tolerance offers a cheap check. Introduced
by \citet{huang1984abft}, ABFT augments a product with checksums and verifies
an invariant that holds in exact arithmetic. For $C = AB$ and a vector of ones
$e$, one compares $Ce$ against $A(Be)$: two paths to the same quantity,
disagreeing only through rounding, unless something went wrong. The check costs
$O(MK + KN)$ against the GEMM's $O(MKN)$.

The difficulty is the threshold. Rounding makes the two paths disagree even
when nothing is wrong, so a tolerance is needed, and the classical tolerance
comes from a bound on dot-product rounding error that requires $Ku \leq 0.01$,
where $u$ is unit roundoff. That condition holds comfortably at float32. It
fails at the precisions that machine learning workloads actually use. At an
embedding dimension of $4096$, $Ku \approx 0.0005$ at float32,
$Ku \approx 4$ at float16, and $Ku \approx 32$ at bfloat16
\citep{ma2025understanding}. Past float32, the threshold is not conservative;
it is undefined.

This has a concrete consequence in the literature. \citet{ma2025understanding},
studying real silent data corruption on unhealthy production nodes, tested
whether checksum ABFT could detect it and ran the test at float32 for exactly
this reason, reporting that at bfloat16 the check produced false positives on
healthy nodes. They concluded that further error analysis is needed to extend
ABFT to low-precision datatypes. That is the gap this paper addresses.

We do not derive a better bound. We measure the distribution the bound was
supposed to describe, and calibrate a threshold from it. Concretely:

\begin{enumerate}
\item We fit a Generalized Pareto distribution to $7.99 \times 10^{7}$ clean
per-row residuals at bfloat16, $K = 4096$, and extrapolate a threshold at a
stated per-GEMM false-positive rate of $10^{-6}$. The method, including the
sensitivity checks and the conditions under which we would declare the
extrapolation unsound, was fixed in writing before the tail was examined.
\item We check the independence assumption behind the per-row to per-GEMM
conversion empirically rather than asserting it.
\item We characterize what the detector catches and misses against that
threshold, stratified by which bit of the bfloat16 word was corrupted and by
how many bits were corrupted at once.
\item We report that detectability does not vary with contraction length across
a $16\times$ range, which falsified the mechanism this study was designed to
demonstrate.
\item We evaluate the detector against a detection bar registered before any
data existed. It fails.
\end{enumerate}

Two things this paper does not claim. It says nothing about the incidence of
faults in real silicon: all corruption here is injected, at the level of the
output tensor, under a model specified in Section~\ref{sec:method:perturbation}.
And it claims no novelty for applying ABFT to transformer-shaped GEMMs, which
is an active area (Section~\ref{sec:related}). The contribution is the
calibration and the characterization, not the detector.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All detection rates in this section are scored against
$\tau = 3.144535556 \times 10^{-6}$ unless stated otherwise, using the
predicate of Section~\ref{sec:method:threshold}: detected when
$r_{\max} > \tau$ or $r_{\max}$ is not finite.

\subsection{The clean noise floor scales as \texorpdfstring{$K^{-1/2}$}{K\^-1/2}}
\label{sec:results:floor}

Across $2500$ clean draws, $500$ at each of five contraction lengths with
$M = N = 4096$ fixed, the median per-row residual falls monotonically with $K$
(Table~\ref{tab:floor}). A log-log fit over the full $16\times$ range gives an
exponent of $-0.4999$; restricted fits give $-0.4997$ over $512$ to $2048$ and
$-0.4991$ over $4096$ to $8192$.

\centering

\midrule

1024 & $7.281700 \times 10^{-7}$ & $5.772809 \times 10^{-6}$ \\
2048 & $5.150956 \times 10^{-7}$ & $3.746226 \times 10^{-6}$ \\
4096 & $3.640184 \times 10^{-7}$ & $2.684525 \times 10^{-6}$ \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Clean noise floor against contraction length. $n = 500$ per $K$.}
\label{tab:floor}
\end{table}

The mechanism is a ratio. Absolute accumulation error in a row sum of $K$
bfloat16 products grows as $\sqrt{K}$, while the per-row normalizer
$s_i = \lvert A_i \rvert \lvert B \rvert e$ grows linearly in $K$. The relative
residual therefore falls as $\sqrt{K} / K = K^{-1/2}$.

We record a correction here. Our locked prediction stated the mechanism as
``clean accumulation error grows as $\sqrt{K}$, signal-to-floor should fall as
$1/\sqrt{K}$.'' The measured exponent matches $\sqrt{K}$ scaling in magnitude,
but that reasoning conflated absolute and relative error and predicted the
wrong direction for the floor's dependence on $K$. The exponent was right for a
reason the argument did not supply.

\subsection{Detectability does not vary with \texorpdfstring{$K$}{K}}
\label{sec:results:kinvariance}

The study was designed to demonstrate that detection degrades as contraction
length grows: a fixed single-element perturbation should become harder to see
as the row sum it hides in gets larger. We registered that prediction, together
with a falsifier stating that if detection were flat across a $16\times$ range
of $K$, the mechanism would be wrong and the finding would reduce to a
single-shape observation with no predictive content.

The falsifier fired. Single sign-bit flips are detected at $79\%$, $75\%$,
$73\%$, $81\%$, and $77\%$ at $K = 512$ through $8192$: a mean of $77\%$ and a
spread of eight percentage points across a $16\times$ range, with no monotone
trend and variation consistent with binomial noise at $n = 100$ (standard error
roughly four points).

The explanation is in Section~\ref{sec:results:floor}. The threshold is derived
from the clean floor at each $K$, and the clean floor falls as $K^{-1/2}$. A
perturbation of fixed magnitude produces a residual that also scales against
the same growing normalizer. Signal and floor move together, so their ratio,
which is what the detector tests, does not depend on $K$. Contraction length
does not govern detectability; bit position does
(Section~\ref{sec:results:bitclass}).

\paragraph{Threshold estimator consistency.}
Detection in this study uses the per-$K$ observed clean maximum as the
threshold, taken over $500$ clean samples at that $K$. An observed maximum is a
sample statistic that grows with $n$ and is therefore not a stable absolute
reference; this is precisely why $\tau$ exists. It does, however, use the same
estimator at the same sample size at every $K$, so the cross-$K$ comparison is
internally consistent even though each individual threshold is unstable in
absolute terms. We state this as a limitation with a defense, not as a claim
that these thresholds are stable.

The stratification spans the full range. Exponent flips are caught most of the
time, sign flips slightly less often, high mantissa flips rarely, and low

we tested. It is a statement about perturbation magnitude relative to the
accumulation floor, expressed through which field of the bfloat16 word was
disturbed, and it does not depend on $K$.

\subsection{Low mantissa flips are a hard negative}
\label{sec:results:mantissa}

MANTISSA\_LOW is detected in zero samples in every cell we ran. Two counts,
which must not be merged because they rest on different thresholds:
$0/1500$ across the K-scaling study scored at per-$K$ observed clean maxima,
and $0/600$ at $\tau$ with $K = 4096$.

At $K = 4096$ the minimum, median, and maximum ratio are identical to four
significant figures at one, two, and four flips ($0.5247$, $0.6217$, $0.827$;
these ratios are to the pilot clean maximum of $3.245921 \times 10^{-6}$, the
basis on which they were originally recorded). More corruption does not produce a larger
residual. A mantissa-LSB perturbation lies below the accumulation floor at this
contraction length regardless of how many of them there are.

We tested whether this was an artifact of the injector rather than a property
of the detector, on the hypothesis that a low-mantissa flip might be rounded
away by the cast back to the working dtype. It is not. Across $50$ samples per
class, zero targeted elements were bitwise identical after the cast and zero
samples had a maximum relative delta of zero; the MANTISSA\_HIGH control
produced relative deltas from $0.0317$ to $0.492$ and MANTISSA\_LOW from
$0.00407$ to $0.0313$. The flips are real and the detector does not see them.

The shared minimum of $0.5247$ across all six mantissa cells is unexplained.
Section~\ref{sec:results:retractions} describes a registered prediction that
would have explained it and could not be tested.

The pre-registered sensitivity check required the refits to agree within a

$1.063\times$. The third could not be evaluated, for reasons in

The per-row target of $2.44 \times 10^{-10}$ follows from a per-GEMM target of
$10^{-6}$ only under independence across the $4096$ rows. Rather than assume
this, we measured the dispersion of per-row exceedance counts: observed mean
$40.42$ and variance $39.93$ per GEMM, against $40.96$ and $40.55$ for
$\mathrm{Binomial}(4096, 0.01)$, a variance ratio of $0.985$. The counts are
slightly under-dispersed relative to independent Bernoulli trials, which
supports the conversion at the fitting threshold. It does not establish
independence in the far tail, and we carry that forward as a stated assumption.
Separately, the mean pairwise correlation between per-row residuals is
$0.000126$.

\subsection{The detector fails its registered bar}
\label{sec:results:kt1}

The bar was written into the repository README before any code was written:
at least $90\%$ of exponent-bit flips detected, at a per-GEMM false-positive
rate below $10^{-6}$. Pooling the two exponent classes at a single flip, the
detector catches $304$ of $400$, or $76\%$, at $\tau$. That is a failure, and
the bar was never moved.

Two points of provenance. First, the same pooled quantity is $301/400$
($75.25\%$) at the pilot observed clean maximum of
$3.245921 \times 10^{-6}$ and $320/400$ ($80\%$) at the K-scaling clean maximum
of $2.684525 \times 10^{-6}$. These differ because an observed maximum is a
sample statistic that grows with $n$, not because any rule or fit changed. We
report $76\%$ as canonical because $\tau$ is the only threshold with a stated
false-positive rate behind it, and we note that $\tau$ falls between the two
sample maxima and is therefore not an artifact of either run.

Second, the verdict was determined before the fit existed. We recorded, in
advance, that the extrapolated threshold was expected to be at or above the
observed clean maximum and that detection is monotone non-increasing in
threshold, so $75.25\%$ bounded detection from above at any defensible
threshold and the bar was already unreachable. The measurement went the
predicted direction: detection fell rather than rose.

\subsection{Retractions}
\label{sec:results:retractions}

Four findings in this project were withdrawn. We report them because a paper
whose principal result is a negative one is only worth reading if the process
that produced it is visible.

\paragraph{One: the scalar reduction.}
An earlier residual summed the length-$M$ checksum vector to a scalar before
comparing. Under it, sign flips and mantissa flips were undetectable in every
cell, $0$ of $1800$ samples, and pooled exponent detection at one flip was
$179/400 = 44.75\%$ with an exact binomial $95\%$ interval of
$[39.8\%, 49.8\%]$. We described the sign and mantissa result as structural
invisibility of the ones-vector checksum. It was not structural. It was the
reduction discarding $M-1$ degrees of freedom before the comparison. Under the
per-row form, sign detection is $155/200$ on the same workload, shape, harness,
and seeds. The earlier evaluation is void and retained in the repository as
evidence that the bar was not moved.

\paragraph{Two: the mantissa near-miss.}
We suspected the MANTISSA\_LOW zero was an injector artifact and tested it. It
was not (Section~\ref{sec:results:mantissa}). The hypothesis is withdrawn and
the hard negative stands.

\paragraph{Three: a prediction falsified at its precondition.}
We registered a mechanism to explain the MANTISSA\_LOW floor and the shared
$0.5247$ minimum: a flip in operand $A$ perturbs both the numerator and the
denominator of Equation~\ref{eq:residual-v3} and may self-cancel, while a flip
in $B$ perturbs the numerator only, so detectability should differ by operand.
We committed four predictions with numeric separations. We then found that the
harness injects into the output tensor only, so no operand asymmetry exists in
any of our data and the predictions are untestable rather than merely
unmeasured. No pass or fail is claimed for them. The $0.5247$ minimum remains
unexplained. Operand-level injection is future work.

\paragraph{Four: a non-monotonicity that was a comparison bug.}
We reported that EXPONENT\_HIGH detection was non-monotone in flip count,
observing $91\%$ at two flips and $85\%$ at four at $K = 1024$, with a similar
inversion at $K = 4096$ and an apparently independent occurrence in a separate
run. Pre-publication audit found the cause. Exponent flips can produce a
non-finite residual, and the analysis path scored detection with a bare
comparison, which evaluates false on a non-finite value. Ten of fifteen
EXPONENT\_HIGH cells contained non-finite residuals, up to $14$ of $100$ at
$K = 1024$ with four flips; no other bit class produced any. Under the correct
predicate, detection is monotone at every $K$: $78/97/100$ at $K = 512$,
$80/96/99$ at $1024$, $88/97/100$ at $2048$, $83/97/100$ at $4096$, and
$83/97/100$ at $8192$. The reported effect does not exist and is withdrawn.
The corroborating run was the same defect, not independent confirmation.

This last one is worth generalizing. An infinite residual is the loudest signal
this detector can emit, and a naive threshold comparison silently discards it.
The hazard applies to any threshold detector operating on floating-point
residuals.

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:scope}

These are boundaries on what was measured, not a list of things we intend to
fix.

\paragraph{Perturbation level.}
All injection is at the output tensor, post-GEMM. Operands, accumulator
intermediates, instruction encoding, and control flow are never perturbed.
Nothing here estimates fault incidence in silicon.

\paragraph{Single configuration.}
One GPU model (Tesla T4, sm75), one workload, one datatype (bfloat16), one
shape family with $M = N = 4096$. The clean floor is per-configuration by
construction and must not be transferred to other hardware.

\paragraph{The p95 fit is not evaluable.}
Our data collection stored the top $64$ per-row residuals per GEMM. At the
$95$th percentile the mean number of exceedances is $205.3$ per GEMM with a
maximum of $263$, so all $19500$ GEMMs are truncated and roughly two thirds of
exceedances are lost, systematically the smaller ones. At the $99$th percentile
only $2$ of $19500$ GEMMs exceed the capture and the effect is negligible: the
shape parameter differs between excluding and right-censoring the truncated
samples by $2.59 \times 10^{-5}$ against a confidence interval width of
$3.90 \times 10^{-3}$, or $0.66\%$ of the interval. This is a data-collection
limitation discovered after the method was locked. It is not a change of
method and not a redefinition of the sensitivity check, which remains as
registered with two of three refits evaluable.

\paragraph{Extrapolation distance.}
The fit reaches from an observed exceedance probability of order $10^{-2}$ to
$2.44 \times 10^{-10}$, several decades beyond the data. The implied upper
endpoints of the fitted distributions differ more than the reported quantiles
do: $3.74 \times 10^{-6}$ from the p99 fit against $4.60 \times 10^{-6}$ from
p99.9, a spread of $1.23\times$, versus $1.06\times$ agreement at the
extrapolated quantile. The hard ceiling on the residual is less well determined
than the sensitivity ratio alone suggests.

\paragraph{Sample sizes.}
Flip cells are $n = 100$ in the K-scaling study and $n = 200$ at $K = 4096$,
giving standard errors of roughly four and three percentage points. Sub-percent
detection probabilities are not resolved.

\paragraph{Unexplained observations.}
The shared minimum ratio of $0.5247$ across all mantissa cells is unexplained
and is reported as an open observation.

\paragraph{Known open issue.}
Two checksum reduction backends in our implementation disagree, one
accumulating in float32 and one in float64. All results reported here use a
single path with float64 accumulation throughout, but the discrepancy is
unresolved and a threshold calibrated on one backend should not be assumed to
transfer to the other.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The headline is a failure, and the useful part is the shape of it.

The detector is close to reliable on exponent corruption and blind to low
mantissa corruption, and no amount of additional corruption in the low mantissa
changes that. This is not a tuning problem. A perturbation that lies below the
accumulation floor is not recoverable by lowering the threshold, because the
threshold is bounded below by the false-positive requirement. The
$76\%$ figure is what this detector achieves at a defensible operating point,
not what it achieves at a favorable one.

Calibrating empirically also changes what the threshold means. The classical
$\tau = Ku$ is a bound: below it, corruption is guaranteed invisible. Ours is a
quantile: above it, false positives occur at a stated rate. The second is
weaker as a guarantee and usable in a regime where the first is undefined. A
practitioner deploying checksum ABFT at bfloat16 today has no principled
threshold available, and the choice is between a calibrated quantile and no
check at all.

The K-invariance result is the one we would flag for anyone extending this. The
intuition that longer contractions hide faults better is natural, we held it
firmly enough to design a study around it, and it is wrong for this detector,
because the normalizer grows with $K$ at the same rate the noise does. Anyone
reasoning about ABFT sensitivity from row-sum magnitude alone should check
whether their normalizer cancels the effect they are predicting.

Finally, the agreement with \citet{ma2025understanding} is worth stating
plainly. They found checksum ABFT failing to catch real corruption on real
unhealthy nodes at float32. We find it catching roughly three quarters of
single exponent flips and none of the low mantissa flips at bfloat16 under
controlled injection with a calibrated threshold. Different corruption sources,
different precisions, different methods, compatible conclusions. The case for
treating checksum ABFT as a complete SDC detector is weak, and the case for
using it as a cheap first filter with known blind spots is what the evidence
supports.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We calibrated a detection threshold for one-sided checksum ABFT at bfloat16
and $K = 4096$, a regime where the classical threshold is undefined, and
characterized what the detector catches and misses against it. It catches
$76\%$ of single exponent-bit flips against a registered bar of $90\%$, and
none of $600$ low-mantissa flips. Detectability is governed by bit position and
not by contraction length, which falsified the mechanism the study was designed
around.

Four registered predictions failed and four findings were retracted, two of
them caught by audit rather than by prediction. We report all of it. The
threshold, the perturbation model, the predictions with their commit
timestamps, and the data are released so that each number here can be checked
against the file that produced it.
